\section{Problem Statement}
\label{intro:sec:problem}

Educators operate under a fixed time budget divided across teaching,
preparation, marking, administration, and --- in higher education ---
research. Survey evidence shows that non-teaching duties consume a large and
stable share of this budget: Singapore teachers report four hours per week of
administrative work (above the OECD average of three) within a 47.3-hour
working week of which only 17.7 hours is teaching, and 53\% rank excessive
administrative work as their foremost source of occupational
stress~\cite{oecd25:TalisSG}. Panel evidence from higher education shows that
this burden is a quality problem, not merely a workload problem: non-teaching
duties that carry no compensating load relief measurably reduce teaching
quality, while research time feeds back positively into teaching quality over
most of its observed range~\cite{apec15:GarciaGallego}. The AI-in-education
literature has long proposed intelligent systems --- chatbots, automated
assessment, learning analytics --- as the remedy, arguing that such
applications \enquote{minimize the administrative tasks of a teacher to
invest more in teaching and guiding students}~\cite{sust22:Ahmad}; yet that
same literature concedes that its claims remain qualitative and
quantitatively unverified, and the systems it surveys predate modern
retrieval-augmented large language models~\cite{sust22:Ahmad,
ijethe19:ZawackiRichter}.

The modern form of the proposed intervention has meanwhile moved from
prototype to production. LLM-based virtual teaching assistants are now
deployed at real institutional scale: Ethel, a retrieval-augmented assistant
at ETH Zurich, served over 1\,500 students across six courses in a single
semester~\cite{tpt24:Kortemeyer}; an LLM-based assistant at KAIST handled
3\,869 interactions from 477 graduate students in one introductory
course~\cite{acl25:Kweon}; and the LLM-powered Jill Watson has been deployed
across multiple institutions and disciplines~\cite{arxiv24:JillWatson}. These
systems share a common architecture --- retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)
over course materials --- and therefore share a common cost structure: every
student query is answered by injecting retrieved course content into the
model's prompt, so the input token count per query is dominated by retrieved
context rather than by the question itself, and that per-query overhead is
multiplied across thousands of interactions per course per
semester~\cite{femnlp23:Liu, acl25:Kweon}.

These costs are material at institutional scale. ETH Zurich reports that
operating Ethel cost US\$7.50 per student per course per semester in cloud
AI-service charges --- at its Spring 2024 scale, five figures per semester for
a handful of courses --- and its team identifies migration toward open-weight,
locally hosted models as a priority for cost
reduction~\cite{tpt24:Kortemeyer}. Local hosting, however, does not make the
cost pressure disappear; it converts it into latency and hardware constraints,
where prompt length directly determines prefill compute on resource-limited
devices. In both regimes, the number of input tokens per query is the primary
controllable cost driver.

This thesis presents a retrieval-augmented virtual teaching assistant built
over NUS Canvas course materials that absorbs routine student-support load
--- clarification, content navigation, and logistics queries --- and, unlike
prior work, instruments the interaction telemetry required to quantify the
instructor-hours it returns, connecting the workload-measurement,
teaching-quality, and AI-intervention literatures for the first time
(\autoref{ch:review}). The system is built for low-cost, locally hosted
deployment: an extractive, sentence-level
retrieval-compression stage adapted from
\textsc{Recomp}~\cite{iclr24:Xu} attacks the input token count at its source,
and the problem, the design, and the evaluation are all measured in the same
units --- tokens, cost, and latency per query
(Chapters~\ref{ch:design} and~\ref{ch:evaluation}).

\section{Objectives}
\label{intro:sec:objectives}

The objective of this thesis is to design and implement a retrieval-augmented generative AI system that addresses the
administrative and pedagogical challenges faced by university faculty. The system aims to automate routine inquiries,
provide personalized feedback to students, and facilitate data-driven course development. By leveraging advanced AI
techniques, the system seeks to enhance faculty efficiency, improve student satisfaction, and foster a more
engaging and effective learning environment. The specific objectives include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item To develop a retrieval-augmented generative AI system capable of handling routine administrative
    \item To implement personalized feedback mechanisms for students based on their performance data
    \item To create a feedback loop that allows faculty to analyze student performance and course effectiveness
    \item To evaluate the system's impact on faculty workload, student satisfaction, and overall course
    \item To provide recommendations for future research and development in the field of AI-assisted education
    \item To ensure the system adheres to ethical guidelines and data privacy standards in educational settings
    \item To explore the scalability and adaptability of the system across different courses and educational contexts
    \item To assess the long-term sustainability and maintenance requirements of the AI system in an academic environment
    \item To investigate the potential for integrating the AI system with existing educational technologies and platforms
    \item To analyze the cost-benefit ratio of implementing the AI system in terms of faculty efficiency and student outcomes
\end{itemize}
