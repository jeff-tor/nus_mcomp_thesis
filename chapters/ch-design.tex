\chapter{System Design and Methodology}
\label{ch:design}
\vspace{2em}

This chapter turns the problem of \autoref{ch:intro} into a design. The
assistant must answer routine student queries well enough to return
instructor time, make that time measurable, and do so cheaply enough to operate at
institutional scale. \autoref{design:sec:requirements} states these needs as
requirements. \autoref{design:sec:deployment} chooses where the system's models
run, \autoref{design:sec:architecture} describes the resulting architecture,
and \autoref{design:sec:methodology} sets out the research questions and how
they are evaluated, including the choice of compression method.

\section{Design Goals and Requirements}
\label{design:sec:requirements}

The requirements follow from the problem statement (\autoref{intro:sec:problem})
and the objectives (\autoref{intro:sec:objectives}), and from the needs of the
system's two user groups: students, who ask questions and practise, and
instructors, whose time the system is meant to return.
\autoref{design:tab:requirements} lists them. Functional requirements (FR)
state what the system must do; non-functional requirements (NFR) state the
constraints it must do it under. Later sections refer to them by identifier.

These requirements pull against each other in two places, and the design
choices that follow are largely about resolving them. Cost (NFR1) and
latency (NFR2) both favour shorter prompts, but answer quality (FR1) favours
more retrieved context; this tension motivates the compression stage
(\autoref{design:subsec:compression-choice}). Cost and modest resources
(NFR1, NFR7) favour running models locally, but latency and quality favour
larger hosted models; this tension is resolved by the deployment decision
(\autoref{design:subsec:deployment-decision}).

\section{Deployment Model Analysis}
\label{design:sec:deployment}

Before committing to an architecture, the system's deployment model must be
chosen: where language-model generation runs, where embedding and retrieval
run, and what happens when either is unavailable. Three candidates are
considered --- fully cloud-hosted, fully on-premises (local), and hybrid ---
and assessed against the requirements of \autoref{design:sec:requirements}.

\autoref{design:tab:deployment} in \autoref{append:comparisons} compares the
three candidate deployment models across eleven dimensions relevant to a
virtual teaching assistant, from scalability and cost to data privacy and
vendor lock-in; the decision below draws on it.

\subsection{Deployment Decision}
\label{design:subsec:deployment-decision}

The system adopts a \emph{hybrid} deployment: generation is served by an
open-weight model family (gpt-oss) on a hosted inference provider (Groq),
embedding and retrieval remain local, and a small local model serves as a
generation fallback. The decision was made empirically. The system first ran
fully locally, and was moved to the hybrid model after a benchmark comparing
local and hosted inference on the same prompts, tools, and tasks
(\autoref{eval:sec:results:providers}).

\paragraph{Why hosted generation.} On this system, hosted generation was
better on every measured dimension except marginal cost, so it best serves
latency (NFR2) and answer quality (FR1, FR5) at an acceptable cost (NFR1). On the flagship
multi-part interaction --- a progress lookup followed by a practice question
--- it reduced median turn latency from 11.2\,s (the local model then in
production) to 1.7\,s, with no loss of grounding fidelity (10/10 turns in
both cases, $n=10$). Hosted models also produced correct quiz answer keys
two to three times as often as the local quiz model, as judged by an AI
model (\autoref{eval:sec:results:providers}). The marginal cost is
US\$0.0003--0.0005 per agent turn, or an estimated US\$0.11--0.27 per
student per semester once retrieved context is included
(\autoref{eval:subsec:results:cost}).

\paragraph{Why not fully local.} A fully local deployment best serves cost
and modest resources (NFR1, NFR7) but fails latency and quality. The local
model capable of reliable grounding (Qwen3, 14B parameters) was five to seven times slower than hosted
generation and, on the 32\,GB host, competed with the retriever for memory:
loading it evicted the embedding model, so every subsequent retrieval paid a
cold reload. The small local model that fits beside the retriever
(Llama~3.2, 3B) is fast enough but weaker, grounding 8/10 multi-part turns
and producing correct quiz keys in 3/10 cases.

\paragraph{Why not fully hosted.} Two requirements rule it out. The chosen
inference provider offers no embedding models, so retrieval with the system's embedder (BGE-M3) must stay
local. A local fallback also converts provider outages, rate limits, and
model retirements into a degradation in answer quality rather than an
outage (NFR4, NFR6) --- a real risk, since a hosted model the system had previously used
was retired by its vendor and broke quiz generation in production.

The trade-off accepted is a dependency on an external provider for the
primary generation path, and a continued dependency on the local host for
retrieval: if the local host is unavailable, the system cannot retrieve
course material even though hosted generation remains reachable.

\section{System Architecture}
\label{design:sec:architecture}

\autoref{design:fig:architecture} shows the deployed system, which has three
tiers:

\begin{figure}[htb]
  \centering
  \input{pic/ch-design/fig-architecture}
  \caption{Deployed architecture of the AI-Powered Omniscient Teacher
           Assistant (hybrid configuration). Solid arrows are live request
           paths; the dashed arrow is the offline ingestion of course
           materials.}
  \label{design:fig:architecture}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Application server} (AWS EC2): runs three components on
        one host. Nginx serves the web application and forwards API
        requests to a Node.js backend (in a Docker container), which handles
        authentication, the tool-calling chat agent, quiz generation, and
        analytics. A PostgreSQL database with the pgvector extension (also
        in Docker) stores users, the question bank, quiz attempts, and the
        course-material chunks and their embeddings.
  \item \textbf{Local inference host} (a 32\,GB Apple M1 Max laptop running
        Ollama, reached from the application server over a reverse SSH
        tunnel): serves the BGE-M3 embedding model used for retrieval and a
        small fallback generation model (Llama~3.2, 3B).
  \item \textbf{Hosted inference provider} (Groq): serves the primary
        generation models, assigned per role --- gpt-oss-20b for the student
        chat agent and gpt-oss-120b for quiz generation
        (\autoref{impl:subsec:llm-providers}).
\end{itemize}

A student query flows through retrieval (the query is embedded with BGE-M3
and matched against course chunks in pgvector), an optional, feature-flagged
compression stage (\autoref{impl:sec:compression}), and generation through a
provider-agnostic layer that routes each call to the hosted provider and
falls back to the local model on failure. Course materials are ingested
ahead of time by an offline job that chunks, embeds, and stores them
(\autoref{impl:sec:retrieval}); the system also connects to Canvas and
Google Classroom over OAuth for course, assignment, and grade data.

The prototype is deliberately minimal: one application host, with the
database in a container rather than a managed service, and sessions managed
by the application. A production-hardened target --- managed database,
container orchestration, a content-delivery network, managed identity, and
scheduled ingestion --- has been designed but not provisioned
(\autoref{ch:concl}).

\section{Methodology}
\label{design:sec:methodology}

\paragraph{Research approach.} The thesis follows a build-and-evaluate
approach: it designs and builds the assistant as an artefact, deploys it on
real course materials, and evaluates it empirically. The
system was developed iteratively, and design decisions were revised when
measurements contradicted them --- the move from a fully local to a hybrid
deployment (\autoref{design:subsec:deployment-decision}) is the clearest
example. Throughout, the problem, the design, and the evaluation are
expressed in the same units --- tokens, cost, and latency per query, and
instructor-hours per course per week --- so that each result can be read
directly against the problem it addresses.

\paragraph{Research questions.} The evaluation answers three questions,
which map onto the objectives in \autoref{intro:sec:objectives}:

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*}, leftmargin=*, nosep]
  \item How can the instructor time the assistant returns be measured in
        hours per course per week, and what must the system record to
        measure it? (Objectives (i)--(ii); FR1--FR3.)
  \item How much does extractive retrieval compression reduce input tokens,
        cost, and latency per query, and at what cost to answer quality?
        (Objective (iv); NFR1, NFR2, FR6.)
  \item Which deployment model --- local, hosted, or hybrid --- best meets
        the cost, latency, and quality requirements? (NFR1, NFR2, NFR4,
        NFR7.)
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Data sources.} Four sources feed the evaluation:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Course materials} from NUS Canvas courses, ingested into the
        retrieval index (\autoref{impl:sec:retrieval}); the compression probe
        and token audit use the CS3241 (computer graphics) corpus, and the
        answer-quality evaluation the IT5001 (Python programming) corpus.
  \item \textbf{Interaction logs} in the production database. Quiz answers
        and feedback notes are already stored; chat turns, which the
        instructor-hours measure needs, are not yet stored on the server
        (\autoref{impl:sec:analytics}).
  \item \textbf{Inference-runtime counters}: prompt and output token counts
        reported by the model runtime for each call, and timings measured
        around each request.
  \item \textbf{Benchmark harnesses} that replay the production prompts and
        agent loop with stubbed tool results, so that models and deployment
        configurations can be compared on identical inputs.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Evaluation methods.} Each research question has its own method,
detailed in \autoref{ch:evaluation}. RQ1 is answered by defining a measure that
aggregates deflected queries and multiplies them by a conservative handling
time per query (\autoref{eval:subsec:hours}), and by identifying the data it
needs; applying it to the deployed system requires chat turns to be stored,
which is left to future work (\autoref{concl:sec:limitations}). RQ2 is a controlled comparison: the same
questions over the same retrieved chunks, with compression switched on and
off by its feature flag, measured on input tokens, cost, end-to-end latency,
and answer quality (\autoref{eval:subsec:tokenmetrics}); the baseline it is
compared against is established by the token audit below. RQ3 is a benchmark
of local and hosted models on identical prompts, tools, and tasks, scored on
latency, grounding fidelity, quiz-answer correctness, and cost
(\autoref{eval:subsec:setup:providers}).

\paragraph{Ethics and data protection.} The evaluation in this thesis uses
no student data. The compression probe uses course materials and questions
written by the author, and the deployment benchmark replaces real student
records with stubbed tool results. Approval from the NUS Institutional
Review Board (IRB) will be sought before any student interaction data --- such
as the chat histories proposed as future work (\autoref{ch:concl}) --- is
collected for analysis. Under the planned data-protection scheme, each
student is registered under a sequential pseudonymous identifier (001, 002,
003, and so on) mapped to their email address, analyses refer to students
only by that identifier, and no personal data beyond what the system needs
to operate is stored.

\subsection{Baseline Token Audit}
\label{design:subsec:tokenaudit}

The literature reviewed in \autoref{review:subsec:tokens} establishes in
general that a RAG pipeline's input token count is dominated by retrieved
context~\cite{femnlp23:Liu}. Whether that holds for \emph{this} system is an
empirical question, and its answer bounds what the compression stage can
save. The audit decomposes the uncompressed pipeline's first-pass prompt into
its components:

\begin{equation}
  \label{design:eq:tokenaudit}
  T_{\mathrm{input}}
    \;=\;
  \underbrace{k \cdot \bar{t}_{\mathrm{chunk}}}_{\text{retrieved context}}
  \;+\; t_{\mathrm{system}}
  \;+\; t_{\mathrm{tools}}
  \;+\; t_{\mathrm{context}}
  \;+\; t_{\mathrm{history}}
  \;+\; t_{\mathrm{query}},
\end{equation}

where $k$ is the number of retrieved chunks per query,
$\bar{t}_{\mathrm{chunk}}$ the mean tokens per chunk under the deployed
chunking configuration, and $t_{\mathrm{system}}$, $t_{\mathrm{tools}}$,
$t_{\mathrm{context}}$, $t_{\mathrm{history}}$, and $t_{\mathrm{query}}$ the
system prompt, the tool definitions, the student's learning-management data,
the conversation history, and the student question respectively. The tool
term is not part of the usual RAG formulation; it is included because the
deployed agent sends its tool definitions with every pass. The quantities of
interest are the baseline input tokens per query and the share attributable
to the retrieved-context term. This baseline anchors the thesis's coherence
loop: the problem statement quantifies cost in tokens per query
(\autoref{intro:sec:problem}), this audit measures the system's baseline in
the same units, and the evaluation reports compression's effect in the same
units again (\autoref{eval:sec:metrics}).

\paragraph{Method.} The audit was run on the deployed system on 19 October
2026, with compression switched off. A script inside the backend container
used the deployed retrieval code, tool definitions, and persona, built each
question's prompt one component at a time, and read the exact input-token
count reported by the hosted provider after each step; the counts are
therefore in the deployed agent model's tokenizer (gpt-oss-20b). Ten
questions over the CS3241 corpus were used: seven standalone in-syllabus
questions of different kinds (definition, concept, equation, detail,
procedure, and synthesis), a follow-up to one of them, a logistics question,
and an off-syllabus question. The tool definitions comprised five tools, two
of them from the connected documentation (MCP) server. The student's
learning-management data was not captured, because no test account is
enrolled in CS3241, so $t_{\mathrm{context}} = 0$ throughout. The script,
questions, and raw results are in the thesis repository
(\texttt{exp/ch-token-audit}).

  \begin{tabular}{l r r}
    \hline
    \textbf{Component} & \textbf{Tokens} & \textbf{Share} \\

\paragraph{Results.} \autoref{design:tab:tokenaudit} shows that, on this
system, retrieved context is not the dominant term. Fixed overhead --- the
tool definitions and the system prompt --- makes up 77\% of the first-pass
prompt, and the tool definitions alone are its largest component. Two
qualifications apply. First, the CS3241 corpus is small: its 314 words form
three chunks of about 171 tokens each, so every in-syllabus question
retrieves the whole corpus. At the configured maximum of $k = 5$, retrieved
context would reach about 850 tokens, or 32\% of the prompt --- still less
than the fixed overhead. Second, the overhead is paid on every agent pass,
and the hosted provider applied no prompt caching
(\autoref{eval:subsec:results:cost}), so a three-pass turn re-sends about
3\,500 tokens of tool definitions alone.

The other three questions show how the prompt varies. The follow-up question
added 310 tokens of conversation history, a term that grows with every turn.
The logistics question retrieved nothing, giving the smallest prompt
(1\,802 tokens). The off-syllabus question on TCP congestion control
retrieved one chunk (213 tokens) that passed the distance cut-off despite
being irrelevant --- the same leak observed in the compression probe
(\autoref{eval:sec:results:compression}), where only the compressor's
sentence threshold withheld it.

\paragraph{Implications.} The audit sets realistic expectations for the
compression stage. Removing about 65\% of the retrieved context, as in the
compression probe, saves about 330 tokens, or 14\% of the first-pass prompt
on this corpus --- much less than the two-thirds of the prompt removed in
the probe, whose prompts (545--769 tokens) carried no tool definitions. It
also identifies a second, larger lever that compression does not touch: the
fixed overhead, and in particular the tool definitions, which could be
reduced by sending only the tools a turn needs (\autoref{ch:concl}).

\subsection{Selection of the Compression Method}
\label{design:subsec:compression-choice}

Among the compression methods surveyed in \autoref{review:subsec:compression},
this thesis adopts the extractive, sentence-level variant of
\textsc{Recomp}~\cite{iclr24:Xu}, for four reasons.

First, fit to the data: course materials are sentence-dense expository text,
and extractive sentence selection preserves the instructor's exact wording
--- important for a study assistant that must not paraphrase away technical
definitions --- whereas abstractive summarisation risks hallucinated
rewording of precisely the content students ask about.

Second, fit to the deployment target: the extractive compressor requires only
dual-encoder sentence scoring, and can reuse the embedding model already
resident on the local host for retrieval, adding no new model. The
alternatives embed a language model in the compression loop --- the
LLMLingua family uses an auxiliary LM for perplexity
scoring~\cite{emnlp23:Jiang, acl24:Jiang}, and ECoRAG relies on LLM-mined
evidentiality labels~\cite{facl25:Jeong}. On this system, such a model would
either compete for memory on the local host, which already cannot hold a
second large model beside the embedder
(\autoref{design:subsec:deployment-decision}), or add a further billed call
to every query on the hosted provider, eroding the saving compression is
meant to deliver.

Third, fit to the pipeline: compression is architecturally a drop-in stage
between retriever and generator, leaving both untouched --- matching the
modular design of \autoref{design:sec:architecture} and enabling the
feature-flagged A/B comparison used in \autoref{ch:evaluation}.

Fourth, evidence of headroom: across the surveyed methods, QA-style workloads
consistently admit 50--95\% token reduction at negligible quality
cost~\cite{femnlp23:Liu, iclr24:Xu, emnlp23:Jiang, acl24:Jiang, emnlp23:Li},
and the material-grounded question answering of a VTA is exactly that
workload shape.

Finally, the choice carries the novelty position stated in
\autoref{review:subsec:compression}: no published work evaluates
\textsc{Recomp} in a deployed educational VTA, so evaluating it here is
itself a contribution rather than a re-implementation. The realisation of
the stage is described in \autoref{impl:sec:compression}.

\section{Summary}
\label{design:sec:summary}

The design rests on three decisions. First, the assistant is a
retrieval-augmented, tool-calling agent over NUS Canvas course materials that
answers routine queries, with a defined measure of the instructor time it
returns (FR1--FR5). Second, it is deployed as
a hybrid: open-weight models on a hosted provider generate answers, while
retrieval and a fallback model run locally --- a choice made on measured
latency, quality, and cost (NFR1--NFR4). Third, an extractive,
\textsc{Recomp}-style compression stage sits between retrieval and generation,
behind a feature flag, to cut input tokens per query and to allow a direct
comparison with and without it (NFR1, FR6). \autoref{ch:implementation}
describes how these decisions were built, and \autoref{ch:evaluation}
evaluates them against the three research questions of
\autoref{design:sec:methodology}.
