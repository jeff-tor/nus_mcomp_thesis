\begin{abstract}

    Educators' working time is heavily taxed by non-teaching duties. In
    Singapore, teachers report that only 17.7 hours of a 47.3-hour working
    week is spent teaching, with four hours consumed by administrative work
    --- their top-ranked source of occupational stress --- and faculty in US
    higher education devote roughly a fifth of a 53-hour week to
    administrative and other non-instructional duties. Econometric evidence
    shows this is a quality problem, not merely a workload problem: when the
    time such duties consume goes uncompensated, teaching quality measurably
    declines. AI-based assistants have long been proposed as the remedy, but
    the workload relief they promise has never been quantified. Meanwhile,
    the modern form of that remedy --- the LLM-based virtual teaching
    assistant, now deployed to thousands of students per semester --- carries
    a cost structure of its own: retrieval-augmented generation answers every
    student query by injecting retrieved course material into the model's
    prompt, so input-token volume, and with it cost and latency, becomes the
    price of deployment at scale.

    This thesis presents the \emph{AI-Powered Omniscient Teacher Assistant},
    a retrieval-augmented virtual teaching assistant built over NUS Canvas
    course materials and designed for low-cost deployment: open-weight
    models generate answers on a hosted inference provider, while retrieval
    and a fallback model run locally.
    It contributes along three fronts. First, it absorbs routine
    clarification, content-navigation, and logistics queries and --- unlike
    prior deployments --- defines a direct, hour-denominated measure of the
    instructor time it returns per course per week, and identifies the
    interaction data needed to compute it. Second, it surfaces analytics over students' quiz
    and assignment results, giving instructors an objective, behaviour-based
    teaching signal alongside timely and consistent responses for students at
    scale. Third, it integrates an extractive, sentence-level
    retrieval-compression stage, adapted from \textsc{Recomp}, between
    retrieval and generation --- attacking the input-token count at its
    source, in what is, to our knowledge, the first evaluation of retrieval
    compression in a deployed educational assistant.

    In a controlled probe on the deployed system, compression removed roughly
    two-thirds of prompt tokens on in-syllabus questions and cut end-to-end
    latency by up to 55\% despite its own overhead, while selective
    augmentation correctly withheld context from an out-of-syllabus query.
    A benchmark of local against hosted inference on the same tasks showed
    hosted generation to be 1.5--6.6 times faster with equal grounding,
    at a projected generation cost of US\$0.11--0.27 per student per
    semester.

\end{abstract}
